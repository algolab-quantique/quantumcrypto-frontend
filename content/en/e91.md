# E91 Protocol Content (Complete Guide)

## About the Protocol

The protocol was proposed in 1991 by Artur Ekert ([Phys. Rev. Lett. 67, 661 (1991)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.67.661)). It involves two distinct parties, Alice and Bob, who wish to establish [an encryption key](definitions#encryption-key) in order to communicate securely over a [public channel](definitions#public-and-private-channel). In this protocol, both Alice and Bob receive a [photon](definitions#photon) from a source that emits pairs of photons whose [polarizations](definitions#polarizations) are [maximally entangled](definitions#maximally-entangled): the photons form what are called [Bell pairs](definitions#bell-pairs). For each pair, the polarization of one photon is measured by Alice and the other by Bob. Alice performs each measurement by randomly choosing a [basis](definitions#measurement-basis) from the set of three bases {a, b, a’}. Bob does the same, choosing from the bases {b, a’, b’}. Among the available measurement bases, Alice and Bob share two: b and a’.

![E91 Measurement Bases](/images/e91-bases.png)

For each measurement, Alice and Bob record the result: +1 or -1. Once the transmission of photon pairs is complete and the measurements are performed, Alice and Bob disclose the measurement bases they used for each photon. The results of [measurements performed in the same basis](definitions#measurements-in-the-same-basis) are kept to form the encryption key. This situation occurs on average 2 times out of 9: when both Alice and Bob measure in basis b or in basis a’. The results of measurements performed in different bases are revealed and used to validate the security of the source and the quantum channel used. To do this, the E91 protocol relies on one of the most spectacular experiments in quantum mechanics: the verification of [Bell inequalities](definitions#bell-inequalities). There are several formulations of these inequalities, and the E91 protocol specifically uses [the CHSH inequality](definitions#chsh-inequality). Alice and Bob will only work with the results of measurements performed in the following bases:

| Alice | Bob |
|-------|-----|
| a     | b   |
| a'    | b   |
| a'    | b'  |
| a     | b'  |

Thus, on average, 4 pairs of photons out of 9 are used to check the CHSH inequality. For each pair of results from the base combinations in the table above, Alice and Bob compute the product $m_A \times m_B$ where $m_A$ and $m_B$ are the measurement results for Alice and Bob, respectively. The average of the products is then calculated for each base combination.

As an example, suppose Alice and Bob performed the following measurements:

| Alice |      | Bob  |      | $m_A \times m_B$ |
|:-----:|:----:|:----:|:----:|:----------------:|
| Base  | $m_A$| Base | $m_B$|                  |
| *a*   | +1   | *b*  | +1   | +1               |
| *a*   | +1   | *b*  | -1   | -1               |
| *a*   | -1   | *b*  | -1   | +1               |
| *a'*  | +1   | *b*  | +1   | +1               |
| *a'*  | -1   | *b*  | +1   | -1               |
| *a'*  | +1   | *b'* | +1   | +1               |

The calculation of the averages (see the definition of [correlation $E(a, b)$](definitions#correlation-ea-b)) gives

$$
E_{a,b} = \frac{1 - 1 + 1}{3} = 0.33
$$

$$
E_{a',b} = \frac{1 - 1}{2} = 0
$$

$$
E_{a',b'} = \frac{1}{1} = 1
$$

$$
E_{a,b'} = 0
$$

The CHSH inequality consists in checking that

$$
S = |E_{a,b} + E_{a',b} + E_{a',b'} - E_{a,b'}| \leq 2
$$

It turns out that when S is calculated from entangled photons, this inequality is not satisfied. In fact, it can be shown that $S = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83$ for maximally entangled photons. Thus, if the photon pair emitter is reliable and the communication channel is not noisy—or worse, eavesdropped!—Alice and Bob should observe that the value of S tends toward $2\sqrt{2}$ as they increase the number of photon pairs considered in their calculation. They can then use the key they obtained to encrypt their message. Conversely, if this value remains below 2, they cannot trust the obtained key and should refrain from using it. Note that average values are statistical quantities that require a large sample to be meaningful. For a limited number of samples, statistical anomalies may occur and it may be difficult to draw conclusions.

Note that there are 3 combinations of measurement bases that are not used in the E91 protocol: (a, a’), (b, b’), and (b, a’). Measurements performed with these base pairs are simply discarded.


## How to play E91
The E91 protocol has two main actors: Alice and Bob, who play different roles. Here you can explore the set of steps that each one of them must take in order to complete the protocol successfully.

### Alice
1. **Randomly pick a measurement basis** for incoming photons, the measurement outcomes will be used to establish your encryption key and detect the presence of Eve.

2. **Share your measurement bases** with Bob.

3. **Extract your encryption key** from the measurement outcomes obtained for identical base pairs.

4. **Validate your key** by testing the CHSH inequality with the measurements taken with the other base combinations.

5. **Encrypt and send your message** to Bob.

### Bob
1. **Randomly pick a measurement basis** for incoming photons, the measurement outcomes will be used to establish your encryption key and detect the presence of Eve.

2. **Share your measurement bases** with Alice.

3. **Extract your encryption key** from the measurement outcomes obtained for identical base pairs.

4. **Validate your key** by testing the CHSH inequality with the measurements taken with the other base combinations.

5. **Decrypt Alice's message** using your key.
